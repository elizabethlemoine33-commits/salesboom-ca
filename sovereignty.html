What laws govern your customers' data in Canada?

Four overlapping frameworks shape how Canadian organizations must handle personal and enterprise data — and the direction of travel is unambiguous: fines are growing, obligations are tightening, and the expectation of genuine sovereignty over data is moving from best practice to legal requirement.

Federal · Since 2001
In Force
PIPEDA — Personal Information Protection and Electronic Documents Act

Canada's primary federal privacy law for private-sector organizations. Requires that personal data be collected, used, and disclosed only with consent, and be protected by appropriate safeguards. Governs any organization handling personal information in the course of commercial activity across provincial lines or outside Canada.

Quebec · Fully in Force Since September 2023
In Force — Strictest in Canada
Quebec Law 25 — Act Respecting the Protection of Personal Information in the Private Sector

The most rigorous privacy law currently in force in Canada. Requires mandatory disclosure of automated decisions affecting individuals, human-review rights, Privacy Impact Assessments for projects involving personal information, and a designated Privacy Officer. Fines up to the greater of C$25 million or 4% of worldwide revenue.

Federal · Introduced June 2026
First Reading — Not Yet Law
Bill C-36 — Protecting Privacy and Consumer Data Act

Proposes to replace PIPEDA with substantially strengthened obligations: data portability rights, algorithmic transparency requirements, consent for automated decision-making, and a new Digital Safety and Data Protection Commission. Maximum fines up to the greater of C$25 million or 5% of global revenue — comparable to Quebec Law 25 and the EU's GDPR.

Federal · Policy Commitment · 2025–2026
Active Framework
Canada's Digital Sovereignty Framework

The Government of Canada has committed investment toward sovereign AI infrastructure and released a Digital Sovereignty Framework that signals risk-based data-transfer assessments and technical controls to reduce foreign-access risk. Direction of travel, not yet binding law — but an unmistakable signal for procurement decisions in government and regulated sectors.

For organizations operating in Quebec, Law 25 compliance is mandatory today. For all other Canadian organizations, PIPEDA applies now and Bill C-36 is the likely successor — designed to enforce obligations that currently require interpretation. The smart procurement decision anticipates the law that is coming, not only the one currently in force.

What is the difference between data residency and data sovereignty?

Data residency means your data is physically stored within Canada's borders. Data sovereignty means it is governed exclusively by Canadian law — including who can compel access to it. The two are not the same, and the gap between them is where most "Canadian hosting" claims fall apart.

Why can't a US-owned vendor fully protect your Canadian enterprise data?

The US Clarifying Lawful Overseas Use of Data (CLOUD) Act, passed in 2018, allows US law enforcement and intelligence agencies to compel any company subject to US jurisdiction to produce data it controls — regardless of where that data is physically stored. If your software vendor has a US parent, your Canadian data is within reach.

This is not a theoretical risk. The CLOUD Act was specifically designed to resolve the jurisdictional gap that existed when companies began storing data offshore. It extends US reach to data stored anywhere in the world, provided the company holding it has a US nexus — through headquarters, incorporation, or a US parent entity.

The distinction that matters: "Stored in Canada" and "not reachable by a US subpoena" are not the same claim. Most enterprise software vendors' marketing blurs them deliberately. A Canadian data centre operated by a US company is still reachable — the server's postal code does not change the corporate structure above it.

For organizations in healthcare, government, defence, financial services, or any sector with fiduciary obligations over personal data, the CLOUD Act exposure that comes with a US-owned CRM vendor is a genuine legal and reputational risk — not a compliance footnote. It is also precisely what Canada's evolving privacy framework is designed to close.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is PIPEDA and does it apply to my organization?
PIPEDA — the Personal Information Protection and Electronic Documents Act — is Canada's federal private-sector privacy law. It applies to any organization that collects, uses, or discloses personal information in the course of commercial activity across provincial or national lines. For most Canadian businesses, PIPEDA governs how you handle customer, employee, and partner data. Quebec-based organizations are also subject to Quebec Law 25, which is currently the most stringent privacy law in the country. Bill C-36, currently at first reading in Parliament, proposes to replace PIPEDA with broader obligations and significantly higher fines.
Does storing data on Canadian servers mean it's protected from US law enforcement?
No. The US CLOUD Act (2018) allows US law enforcement to compel any company subject to US jurisdiction to produce data it controls, regardless of where that data is physically stored. If your CRM vendor has a US parent entity — as Amazon Web Services, Microsoft Azure, Google Cloud, Salesforce, and HubSpot do — your data on their Canadian servers is still reachable by a US court order. True sovereignty requires that the vendor itself, not just the server's location, sits outside US legal reach. Salesboom has no US parent entity and no US-owned infrastructure in its hosting chain.
What is Quebec Law 25 and what are the CRM-specific obligations?
Quebec Law 25 (Act Respecting the Protection of Personal Information in the Private Sector) is fully in force as of September 2023 and is the strictest privacy law currently operating in Canada. For organizations using CRM software, its most material requirements are: disclosure of any automated decision-making that affects individuals (lead scoring, opportunity weighting, and pipeline AI all qualify), the right of individuals to request human review of automated decisions, mandatory Privacy Impact Assessments before deploying new personal-information processing, and appointment of a designated Privacy Officer. Fines reach the greater of C$25 million or 4% of worldwide revenue.
What is Canada's Digital Sovereignty Framework?
The Government of Canada has committed investment toward sovereign AI infrastructure and released a Digital Sovereignty Framework that signals a shift toward risk-based data-transfer assessments and technical controls designed to limit foreign governments' access to Canadian data. While not yet binding law, it represents the direction of federal procurement policy and is influencing how government-adjacent organizations — and those seeking federal contracts — approach vendor selection. Combined with Bill C-36 and the strengthening US–Canada trade context, it makes Canadian ownership a strategic procurement consideration, not only a compliance one.
